One sack at a time: why we made this page

Why we made it

Every day, the Philippines produces about 61,700 tons of garbage. (Philippine Information Agency, 2026) Too much of it ends up where it should not: in open dumps, vacant lots, canals, and the smoke of burning trash. We made this page because these facts are easy to ignore when they are scattered. Here they are in one place, in plain words, with their sources.

What we stand for

Lupang Iingatan means “the land we will protect.” About 95% of our food is produced in soil, it takes more than 1,000 years to form just 1 centimeter of it, and soil is home to a quarter of all life on Earth. (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO], 2017) When we poison the land, we cannot simply make more. So this page stands for three things:

  • Sorted at the source. Every home separates its waste before it leaves the house.
  • Laws that work. RA 9003 is enforced, not just written down.
  • Producers that take back. Companies help recover the packaging they sell.

How we want you to join

We are not asking you to save the planet. We are asking you to start with one sack. Read the posts, try one habit this week, take the pledge at the bottom of this page, and share it with one person. That is how a barangay changes: one household at a time.

What We Learned

Lupang IingatanBlog

What the numbers told us

On the What is the Issue? and What to do? pages, we collected data on land pollution and on the solutions already in place. Put together, the numbers say three things.

Garbage is growing faster than our solutions.

Daily waste rose from about 40,087 tons in 2016 to about 61,700 tons in 2026, an increase of roughly 54%. (Senate Economic Planning Office, 2017; Philippine Information Agency, 2026) In a single year, materials recovery facilities grew by 8.7%, while reported illegal dumpsites grew by 84%. (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025)

The law is strong. The follow-through is weak.

RA 9003 has required waste segregation since 2001. Today, 92% of LGUs have ten-year waste plans, yet only about 53% of the country’s waste ends up in sanitary landfills. (Daily Tribune, 2026a) At home, 1 in 5 households say they never segregate, and 27% get rid of their trash by burning it, which the law prohibits. (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2023)

Most of the fix starts at home.

Households produce 57% of the country’s solid waste, and 80% of what we throw away is biodegradable or recyclable. (Senate Economic Planning Office, 2017) An MRF can only recover what reaches it sorted. When waste arrives mixed, most of it ends up in a landfill or a dump.

Our conclusion. Laws do not sort garbage. People do. The system built under RA 9003 will only work when every household hands over waste that is already sorted, and when leaders are held to their own plans.

Out of every 100 kilos we throw away

Only 18 kilos truly need a landfill. The rest could become compost, new products, or safely handled special waste, if we sort it first.

If every home sorted, 8 of every 10 kilos would never need a landfill. That is the cheapest landfill space there is: the space we never fill.

We have seen this before

  1. July 2000

    A mountain of garbage at the Payatas dumpsite collapsed onto the homes around it and killed more than 200 people. The public outrage helped push a waste law through within months. (Agence France-Presse, 2026) RA 9003 was signed on January 26, 2001. (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001)

  2. January 2026

    A huge mound of garbage at the Binaliw landfill in Cebu City gave way and buried workers and buildings. When the search ended ten days later, 36 people had died and 18 were hurt. (Cebu Daily News, 2026)

  3. April 2026

    A fire at the inactive Navotas Sanitary Landfill raised new concerns about the safety of waste sites. The DENR then told all LGUs to stop operating open and controlled dumpsites immediately. (Manila Bulletin, 2026)

Twenty-six years apart, the lesson is the same. Every sack we do not sort adds to a mountain somewhere, and someone lives or works beside it. Sending less waste to these sites is the most basic kind of disaster prevention. We remember the workers and families who lost their lives.

What You Can Do

Lupang IingatanGuide

Ako, Tayo, Sila: who must do what

Protecting the land is not one person’s job, but it starts with one person. Here is what each circle must do, from the home to the halls of government.

Ako I, at home

  • Sort into four. Biodegradable, recyclable, residual, and special. Segregation at source is required by law, not just encouraged. (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001)
  • Compost food scraps. Half of our garbage is food and garden waste. (Senate Economic Planning Office, 2017) A pot, a pit, or a barangay compost area turns it back into soil. See how in the composting video below.
  • Skip the sachet when you can. Filipinos use an estimated 163 million sachets a day, and most are hard to recycle. (World Bank Group, 2021) Buy bigger packs, or bring a container to a refill store.
  • Never burn or dump trash. Burning releases toxic smoke, and dumped waste seeps into soil and water. Both are illegal, with fines of ₱300 to ₱1,000 and community service or jail time. (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001)
  • Keep special waste apart. Batteries, bulbs, paint, old gadgets, and used oil need special handling. The country produced 269.55 thousand tons of hazardous waste in 2024, and oil was the largest share. (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025)

Tayo We, in the barangay and school

  • Follow the collection schedule. Put out each type of waste on its own collection day, so sorted waste is not mixed again in the truck.
  • Ask about your MRF. RA 9003 requires one in every barangay or cluster of barangays. (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001) At the next barangay assembly, ask whether yours is working and who runs it.
  • Make clean-ups count. Sort what you collect during a clean-up drive, so it goes to the MRF and not back to a dump.
  • Start at school. Sixteen schools lead the zero waste program of San Fernando, Pampanga, a city that has cut the waste it sends for disposal by about 80%. (ABS-CBN News, 2019) A compost pit and a garden make a good first project.

Sila They, leaders and companies

These are not ours to do, but they are ours to demand.

  • LGUs must close dumpsites and show results. The DENR has ordered all open and controlled dumpsites to stop operating. (Manila Bulletin, 2026) Ask your city or municipality to publish the yearly results of its ten-year plan, so citizens can check whether it works.
  • Barangays must enforce, fairly. Collect only sorted waste, keep the MRF running, and act on reports of dumping and burning.
  • Companies must take back their packaging. Under the EPR Act, large companies must recover 60% of their plastic packaging footprint in 2026 and 80% from 2028. (Republic Act No. 11898, 2022) In 2025, registered programs diverted about 246 million kilos of plastic. (Daily Tribune, 2026b) Ask the brands you buy what they do with their sachets.
  • Everyone can report. If you see illegal dumping or open burning, report it to your barangay, your city or municipal environment office, or the DENR-EMB regional office. Note the place and time, take a photo if it is safe, and follow up.

Why this protects the whole ecosystem. Clean soil keeps doing its quiet work: growing our food, filtering our water, and housing a quarter of Earth’s living things. (FAO, 2017) Every action above protects that work.

Lupang IingatanShared reads

Three things worth your time

We read and watched a lot for this project. These three are the ones we would hand to a friend first.

  • Documentary from GMA Public Affairs, 2024 Republika ng Plastik (The Atom Araullo Specials) A full documentary on the country’s growing plastic crisis and what it does to nature, livelihoods, and health. It asks a hard question: can we solve a problem made of something that lasts almost forever? Watch it with your family.
  • Report from the World Bank Group, 2021 Market Study for the Philippines: Plastics Circularity Opportunities and Barriers Our trash is also wasted money. The country generates 2.7 million tons of plastic waste a year, and about 78% of the value of key plastics, more than US$890 million a year, is lost when they are thrown away instead of recycled.
  • Report from the United Nations Environment Programme, 2024 Global Waste Management Outlook 2024 The world picture. In 2020, 38% of the world’s municipal waste was dumped or openly burned, and the yearly total is expected to grow from 2.1 to 3.8 billion tons by 2050. The Philippines is not alone in this problem, and it is not alone in the solutions either.
